# Accuracy check — the main claims and the disputed points

For interest: every stop text was written from opened sources; these are the points where sources disagree, plus anything the researchers could not verify. GPS: see gps-check.csv (9 pins moved after an independent check against OpenStreetMap).

## Where the sources disagree (shown in the app on each stop)

- **Fort Eben-Emael** — Garrison figures vary: about 1,200 on strength but perhaps 750 present on 10 May (Witzig's figure), while some books give 1,185 or even 2,000. Wikipedia's fort article gives the glider force as both 85 and 78 men.
- **Bastogne Barracks (McAuliffe's HQ)** — The barracks is spelled Heintz in most sources but Heinz in some veterans' material.
- **Bois Jacques foxholes, Foy** — The memorial text says Easy Company dug in on 18 December, but the 101st Airborne only reached Bastogne on 19 December; the line was probably occupied a day or two later.
- **December 44 Historical Museum, La Gleize** — Peiper's figure of 717 survivors out of 3,000 is his own testimony; the battle group's strength is variously given, up to 4,800 men. The Tiger is said to have been saved from scrapping by a local woman's gift of a bottle of cognac to the Americans, a local story rather than a documented fact.
- **Baugnez: Malmedy massacre memorial** — Wikipedia's Baugnez article speaks of 'more than 70' victims and 72 stones in the wall, while the memorial and most accounts give 84 dead. Who gave the order to fire was never established at the trial; witnesses named Werner Poetschke, while Peiper bears command responsibility.
- **Houffalize and its Panther tank** — Accounts differ on how the Panther ended up in the river: a driver's error or the partial collapse of the bridge. One source says the crew's bodies were recovered in 1947, another that the tank was raised by Namur engineers on 20 September 1948.
- **Recogne German war cemetery** — Sources differ on when the American dead were moved: Wikipedia says 1945–46, TracesOfWar summer 1948.
- **Henri-Chapelle American Cemetery** — Burial totals differ slightly between sources (7,989 on TracesOfWar, 7,992 on Wikipedia and Liberation Route).
- **Luxembourg American Cemetery, Hamm** — Burial totals vary slightly: Wikipedia gives 5,074 and Visit Luxembourg 5,076.
- **Battle of Britain Bunker, Uxbridge** — Churchill's 'so few' line is often placed on 15 September; the bunker's own history says he first said it leaving Uxbridge on 16 August 1940, before the Commons speech of 20 August.
- **IWM Duxford** — The 'Big Wing' of three to five 12 Group squadrons, assembled at Duxford in September 1940, remains debated: supporters credited it with large claims, while critics, Park among them, said it formed up too slowly and its claims were inflated.
- **Dover Castle Secret Wartime Tunnels** — Totals vary by source: 338,226 is the usual figure for Dynamo, but counts that include earlier evacuations give higher numbers. Sources also differ on whether Ramsay was Vice-Admiral (his rank in 1940) or Admiral (later).
- **St Paul's Cathedral and the Blitz, London** — Churchill's order to save St Paul's at all costs is widely repeated but rests on later accounts ('reportedly'); no written order is known.
- **Coventry Cathedral ruins** — A long-running claim says Churchill knew of the raid from Ultra decrypts and let Coventry burn to protect the secret; historians who have studied the records reject it, as the target was not identified in time.
- **Bletchley Park** — The claim that Bletchley shortened the war by two years comes from official historian Sir Harry Hinsley; others think the effect cannot be measured so precisely.
- **Lidice Memorial** — Figures vary by source: 173 men were shot at Lidice on 10 June according to most counts, while other accounts give 199 men killed in total including those executed elsewhere; about 340 villagers died overall.
- **Operation Anthropoid Memorial, Prague-Libeň** — Heydrich's exact cause of death is debated: the autopsy pointed to sepsis from wound fragments; others propose an embolism, and a fringe claim that the grenade carried botulinum toxin is rejected by most historians.
- **Terezín Memorial** — Totals vary: about 141,000–154,000 people passed through the ghetto depending on how late arrivals and repeat prisoners are counted, and onward deportations are given as 88,000–90,000.
- **Mauthausen Memorial** — Because records were destroyed and prisoner numbers reused, death totals for Mauthausen and its subcamps range from 'more than 90,000' to about 120,000 depending on the source.
- **Gusen Memorial** — Death figures range from the 27,842 in official records to estimates of 35,000–37,000 or more, because unregistered prisoners killed on arrival are missing from the records.
- **Malo-les-Bains evacuation beach, Dunkirk** — Popular memory credits the 'little ships' and the beaches with the rescue, but the official figures show about 239,555 men left from the harbour against 98,671 from the beaches, mostly in destroyers and larger vessels.
- **Fort des Dunes, Leffrinckoucke** — The commonly quoted toll of 150–200 dead in the June 1940 bombing is challenged by burial records suggesting nearer a hundred; sources also differ on whether seven or eight resistance fighters were shot here in September 1944.
- **Dunkirk Memorial and Town Cemetery (CWGC)** — CWGC lists 4,509 identified casualties on the memorial; the Dunkirk tourist office guide gives 4,528.
- **Zuydcoote and Bray-Dunes beach wrecks** — Casualty figures for the Crested Eagle do not reconcile: about 600 aboard, about 200 rescued and a BBC-cited figure of about 300 killed.
- **La Plaine au Bois massacre memorial, Esquelbecq (Wormhout)** — Victim numbers vary: about 80 killed outright, with sources giving 80 or 81 dead and differing on how many of the wounded later died.
- **Batterie Todt (Atlantic Wall Museum), Audinghen** — Sources differ on when the battery first fired: English Wikipedia gives 20 January 1942, while French Wikipedia cites a 1940 German press report of a shell fired during construction.
- **Brest Fortress, Belarus (June 1941)** — Soviet accounts from the 1950s, popularised by Sergei Smirnov, said the fortress held out for a month and that nobody surrendered. Modern research finds organised resistance ended around 29 June, with only scattered holdouts after that.
- **Piskaryovskoye Cemetery, St Petersburg** — Burial figures vary: Wikipedia gives about 420,000 civilians and 50,000 soldiers, the Memorial Museums portal 470,000 residents, and a plaque on site cites 641,803 starvation deaths for the whole city. There is no complete register of those buried.
- **Museum of the Defence and Siege of Leningrad** — Sources disagree on the dates of its first life: the St Petersburg Encyclopaedia gives closure in August 1949 and liquidation in 1952, while some guides give closure in 1952 or 1953.
- **Panorama Museum & Gerhardt's Mill, Volgograd** — The mill is named after its pre-revolutionary owner Gerhardt and after Grudinin, a Soviet-era name; sources use both. Russian Wikipedia gives the defenders as the 42nd Rifle Regiment, TracesOfWar as the 42nd Guards Rifle Regiment.
- **Pavlov's House, Volgograd** — Who commanded is debated: the house is named after Sergeant Pavlov, but some historians credit Lieutenant Afanasyev. The start date (23 or 27 September) and the length of the defence (58 or 60 days) vary, and German records do not support claims of very heavy assaults on the house.
- **Rossoshka war cemeteries** — Figures for the German cemetery differ: the Volksbund lists 61,791 buried (71,427 total recorded), Wikipedia about 65,000 after later reburials, and Volgograd State University's guide 'more than 50,000'.
- **Prokhorovka battlefield, Belgorod region** — Soviet histories called Prokhorovka 'the greatest tank battle in history' and a decisive victory. Modern research (Zamulin, Frieser, Glantz) shows far fewer tanks than claimed and much heavier Soviet than German losses; the battle stopped the German advance but was a tactical Soviet defeat.
- **Khatyn Memorial, Belarus** — Khatyn is often confused with Katyn, where the NKVD shot Polish officers in 1940; historian Norman Davies argues the Soviets chose Khatyn for a national memorial partly to exploit that confusion. Child victims are given as 70 or 75 depending on the count.
- **Reichstag, Berlin** — Khaldei's 2 May photograph was staged the day after the building was taken and retouched before publication; accounts also differ over which soldiers first raised a flag on 30 April.
- **Museum Berlin-Karlshorst** — Because the Karlshorst signing took effect just after midnight Moscow time, Russia marks Victory Day on 9 May while the West marks 8 May.
- **Seelow Heights Memorial** — Soviet losses are disputed: Hastings and Beevor put Soviet dead at 30,000–33,000, while Russian historian Aleksey Isaev, using archives, gives 5,000–6,000 killed and missing.
- **Sachsenhausen Memorial and Museum** — The death toll is uncertain because records were destroyed: historians estimate at least 30,000, while commandant Anton Kaindl acknowledged 42,000 deaths during his time.
- **Buchenwald Memorial, near Weimar** — The memorial gives 56,545 deaths, but the Soviet special camp death toll is variously given as 7,113 or 7,127, and the GDR's heroic account of communist self-liberation has been revised since 1990.
- **Colditz Castle** — Popular accounts of the number of 'home runs' from Colditz vary, often around 30 or more, depending on whether escapes from hospitals or during transfers are counted.
- **Torgau: the Elbe Day meeting** — Torgau is remembered as the meeting place, but Kotzebue's patrol made the first documented contact earlier the same day near Strehla; the best-known photographs were staged on 26 April.
- **Dresden Frauenkirche and the 1945 bombing** — Nazi propaganda claimed 200,000 dead and later deniers higher still; a 2010 Dresden historians' commission found 22,700 to 25,000. Whether the raid was militarily justified is still debated.
- **St Nikolai Memorial, Hamburg** — Estimates of deaths range from 34,000 (police records) to 40,000 (a wartime German figure); 37,000 is the most widely accepted.
- **Peenemünde Historical-Technical Museum** — The site is often presented as the birthplace of space flight; critics stress that more people died building the V-2 than were killed by it.
- **Ravensbrück Memorial** — Death toll estimates vary widely; Britannica cites about 50,000 plus some 2,200 gassed, while the memorial's own figures are lower.
- **Museum Hürtgenwald 1944, Vossenack** — American losses are given anywhere from about 33,000 to 55,000 depending on whether non-combat casualties (trench foot, exposure, combat fatigue) are counted. Historians such as Charles MacDonald judged the battle misconceived; others defend the need to clear the forest and reach the Rur dams.
- **Remagen bridge and Peace Museum** — The number of US engineers killed in the collapse on 17 March is given as 28 in some accounts and 32 in others, and the injured as 63 or 93. Why the German charges failed (weak industrial explosive, a cut cable or a dud) is still argued.
- **Dachau Concentration Camp Memorial Site** — Death figures differ: the memorial cites about 41,500, while documented deaths in the archive number about 32,000 and many more are undocumented. The number of SS guards killed by liberating US soldiers and prisoners on 29 April 1945 is also disputed, with estimates from about 21 to 50.
- **Kehlsteinhaus (Eagle's Nest)** — Who got there first in May 1945 is argued: the 101st Airborne claimed it (repeated in 'Band of Brothers'), the US 3rd Infantry Division's 7th Infantry Regiment and the French 2e Division Blindée both entered Berchtesgaden on 4 May, and French soldiers were at the Kehlsteinhaus on 4–5 May.
- **Abbey of Monte Cassino** — Whether German troops were in the abbey before the bombing is long disputed. Some Allied observers claimed they were, but the Germans had agreed with the Vatican not to occupy it, and the British official history and a 1969 US Army review concluded they made no military use of it before 15 February.
- **Polish War Cemetery, Monte Cassino** — Sources give different totals: Wikipedia states 1,072 Polish graves, while Italy's official tourism site counts 1,051 crosses.
- **Anzio Beachhead Museum (Museo dello Sbarco)** — Mark Clark's decision on 25–26 May to turn north-west for Rome rather than cut Route 6 at Valmontone is still argued over; Truscott and others held that it let much of the German Tenth Army escape.
- **Fosse Ardeatine Mausoleum, Rome** — How much the Vatican knew in advance is debated: a Vatican note on the morning of 24 March expected a ten-for-one reprisal, and historians such as Robert Katz have argued Pius XII did too little, which his defenders reject.
- **Maleme German War Cemetery and Hill 107, Crete** — Andrew's withdrawal from Hill 107 is still argued over: some blame him, others the brigade and division commanders who did not reinforce him or counter-attack quickly. Historians also differ on how much the Ultra intelligence that warned of the attack could have helped Freyberg.
- **Galatas, Crete: the battle for the village** — Sources differ on the tanks' unit: NZHistory names 7th Royal Tank Regiment, while most accounts, including Farran's own, place him in the 3rd Hussars.
- **Rotterdam 1940: 'The Destroyed City' monument** — Allied wartime propaganda claimed some 30,000 dead; the true toll was far lower (about 900 by older counts, with a 2022 official list giving at least 1,150 overall). Whether Kesselring's staff knew the raid had been called off before it went in is still argued.
- **Grebbeberg battlefield and military cemetery** — German losses are given variously: an official figure of 238 killed, with estimates of 250–300.
- **Anne Frank House, Amsterdam** — Who betrayed the hiding place is unknown; several theories, including a 2022 'cold case' book naming Arnold van den Bergh, have been strongly contested.
- **Waal crossing memorial (De Oversteek), Nijmegen** — Whether the Guards Armoured Division should have pushed on to Arnhem on the night of 20 September, instead of halting at Lent, is still argued between American and British accounts.
- **Groesbeek Canadian War Cemetery and Memorial** — The number of names on the Groesbeek Memorial is given as 1,016 (Wikipedia, citing CWGC) and 1,047 (TracesOfWar).
- **Overloon War Museum** — Casualty figures for the battle are inconsistent between sources, and its nickname 'the second Caen' is unsourced; it is also called 'the forgotten battle'.
- **Camp Westerbork Memorial Centre** — Deportee totals differ: Wikipedia gives 97,776, while the memorial has 102,000 stones and the centre says over 100,000; figures depend on which transports and groups are counted.
- **Pegasus Bridge and Memorial Pegasus** — Lieutenant Den Brotheridge, killed crossing the canal bridge, is often called the first Allied soldier killed by enemy fire on D-Day; other airborne men died at about the same time, so the claim cannot be proved. The Café Gondrée's claim to be the first house liberated in France is similarly a tradition.
- **Merville Battery** — Sources differ on how much damage the paratroopers did to the guns and whether the battery fired again later on 6 June; the guns were also much smaller than the 150 mm weapons the casemates were built for.
- **Arromanches: Mulberry harbour and D-Day Museum** — Figures for what Port Winston handled vary widely, even within the same sources: 'over two million' to 'almost three million' men, four million tons of stores and half a million vehicles, and its working life is given as anything from six to ten months.
- **Longues-sur-Mer battery** — Accounts differ on which ships silenced the battery: British sources credit HMS Ajax, while others credit the French cruisers Georges Leygues and Montcalm, supported by USS Arkansas.
- **Omaha Beach: Les Braves, Saint-Laurent-sur-Mer** — Casualty figures for Omaha vary widely between sources, commonly from about 2,000 to over 4,000 killed, wounded and missing, depending on what is counted and when.
- **Pointe du Hoc** — A popular myth says the assault was pointless because the guns had gone. In fact the guns were nearby and could still reach the beaches, and the Rangers found and disabled them on the morning of D-Day.
- **Sainte-Mère-Église and the Airborne Museum** — John Steele's story has been embellished over the years, and the film The Longest Day placed his parachute on the wrong side of the tower; the dummy now hangs on the side that suits the square, not necessarily where he was caught.
- **Utah Beach Museum, Sainte-Marie-du-Mont** — Roosevelt's words on finding the troops off course are usually quoted as 'We'll start the war from right here', but the exact wording varies between accounts.
- **Montormel Memorial, Falaise Pocket** — Estimates of how many Germans escaped from the pocket vary greatly between sources, from about 20,000 to over 50,000.
- **Vemork: Norwegian Industrial Workers Museum** — The date of the Gunnerside attack is given as the night of 27/28 February 1943 in many accounts but as 28 February/1 March 1943 on Wikipedia's Vemork page.
- **Sedan: the Meuse crossing, May 1940** — Figures for Guderian's armour differ (771 or 788 tanks), and accounts disagree on whether Sedan town itself was taken without resistance on 12 May.
- **Reims Surrender Museum (Musée de la Reddition)** — Russia and the West still date the end differently: the Reims signing of 7 May took effect on 8 May, but the Soviet Union treated the Berlin ratification late on 8 May (9 May Moscow time) as the real surrender.
- **Natzweiler-Struthof** — English Wikipedia gives about 22,000 deaths in the camp and subcamps; French Wikipedia gives about 17,000 plus about 5,000 on the evacuation marches, and notes many executions were never recorded.
- **Westerplatte, Gdańsk** — The garrison was long given as 182 men; recent research suggests 210–240. Historians also debate Sucharski's conduct: some accounts say he broke down and urged early surrender, leaving Dąbrowski effectively in command.
- **Polish Post Office, Gdańsk** — In 1998 a court in Lübeck annulled the 1939 death sentences, partly because the military penal law used had not yet come into force in Danzig when the defenders were tried.
- **Museum of the Second World War, Gdańsk** — After 2016 the government merged the museum with the Westerplatte museum and removed its founding director; new management changed parts of the exhibition. In 2020 the original authors won a copyright case over changes made without consulting them.
- **Warsaw Rising Museum** — Historians disagree on why the Red Army halted on the Vistula: most Polish and Western writers argue Stalin let the Germans destroy the Home Army; others, such as David Glantz, argue the Soviets were genuinely stopped by German defences.
- **Ghetto Heroes Monument and POLIN Museum, Warsaw** — The ŻZW's role was long played down in Soviet-era and some Israeli accounts that presented the ŻOB as the sole force. Stroop reported 16 Germans killed; historians such as Israel Gutman think the real figure was higher.
- **Treblinka Museum and memorial** — Estimates of the number murdered at Treblinka II vary from about 800,000 to 925,000, because the Germans destroyed the records; Wikipedia also gives inconsistent figures for Treblinka I.
- **State Museum at Majdanek, Lublin** — The death toll has been revised sharply: from 360,000 (1948) and 235,000 (1992) to the museum's 2005 estimate of about 78,000, including 59,000 Jews, based partly on the Höfle Telegram.
- **Sobibór Museum and memorial** — Estimates of the number murdered range from about 170,000 to 250,000.
- **Bełżec Museum and memorial** — Estimates vary: Wikipedia's text gives 430,000–500,000, its infobox 434,508–600,000, and historians from about 480,000 to 550,000. The figure of 434,508 comes from the Höfle Telegram up to the end of 1942.
- **Auschwitz I (main camp), Oświęcim** — The gas chamber and crematorium at Auschwitz I were partly reconstructed by the museum after the war, a fact the museum states openly; Holocaust deniers misuse it.
- **Auschwitz II-Birkenau** — During the communist period plaques at Birkenau gave four million victims; research led by the museum's historian Franciszek Piper established about 1.1 million, and the plaques were replaced in the 1990s.
- **Oskar Schindler's Enamel Factory, Kraków** — Schindler's motives remain debated: he was a profiteer and Abwehr agent who first hired Jews as cheap labour. Several versions of 'Schindler's list' exist because it was retyped as conditions changed.
- **Brest: the U-boat base and the Naval Museum** — Sources give different dates for the end of the siege, from 18 to 23 September 1944, depending on whether they count the city, Ramcke's own surrender or the last outposts. The cause of the Sadi-Carnot explosion is also uncertain.
- **Keroman submarine base, Lorient** — The surrender of the Lorient pocket is given as 8 May 1945 by uboat.net; other accounts place the formal handover on 10 May.
- **Saint-Nazaire: U-boat base and Normandie dock** — Figures for the raid differ: Wikipedia gives 612 men and 169 killed, while the Falmouth memorial gives 611 and 168 in its text and 622 on its plaque.
- **Oradour-sur-Glane: ruined village and Centre de la mémoire** — The death toll is usually given as 642; a 2020 study identified a 643rd victim. The trigger for the massacre, the capture of Kämpfe, is debated as a motive.

## The big disputes (Briefing page)

- **How many Soviet dead?** — The Soviet Union lost about 27 million people, soldiers and civilians, according to the figure published in 1990 from demographic calculations; Stalin had admitted 7 million, Khrushchev 20 million. For military deaths, the Russian General Staff study led by G. F. Krivosheev gives about 8.7 million, including prisoners who died in captivity. Some Russian and Western researchers argue that this is too low and that military dead may have exceeded 11 million. Civilian deaths are harder still to count, because the borders of 1941 and the population of the occupied lands are themselves uncertain.
- **The halt before Dunkirk** — On 24 May 1940 Hitler confirmed a halt of the panzers short of Dunkirk, which gave the Allies time to organise the perimeter and the evacuation. Why is still argued. The halt was proposed by Rundstedt, commanding Army Group A, to rest and repair his tanks for the battle still to come in the south and to avoid the canals and marshes round Dunkirk; Göring promised that the Luftwaffe could finish the job. The idea that Hitler let the British go to make peace easier comes mainly from later claims and is doubted by most historians.
- **How big was Prokhorovka?** — Soviet histories described Prokhorovka on 12 July 1943 as the greatest tank battle in history, with some 1,500 tanks and a German armoured force smashed. Research in German and Soviet archives since the 1990s — by Karl-Heinz Frieser, Valeriy Zamulin, David Glantz and others — shows roughly 600 Soviet tanks and self-propelled guns against fewer than 300 German. The Soviets lost around 300–400 tanks that day; German permanent losses were small, perhaps only a handful, though many more were damaged. The Soviet attack nevertheless stopped the SS Panzer Corps, and the offensive was called off.
- **The Dresden death toll** — For decades, figures of 135,000, 200,000 or even more were quoted for the dead of the February 1945 raids on Dresden. They began with Nazi propaganda, which added a nought to a police report, and were spread after the war, notably by David Irving on the basis of a forged document. A historians' commission set up by the city reported in 2010 that between 18,000 and 25,000 people died. The moral argument over the raid — military necessity or terror bombing — continues separately from the numbers.
- **Katyn** — When Germany announced the discovery of the Katyn graves in April 1943, the Soviet Union blamed the Germans, broke off relations with the Polish government in London when it asked for a Red Cross inquiry, and in 1944 produced its own commission report. The Soviets even raised Katyn at the Nuremberg trials, where the charge was quietly dropped. Western governments, needing Stalin, said little. Only in 1990 did the Soviet Union admit that the NKVD had carried out the killings; some Russian investigation files remain closed, and the full list of victims from Belarus has never been published.
- **Why did the Red Army stop at Warsaw?** — When the Home Army rose in August 1944, the Red Army stood a few kilometres from Warsaw and then halted. One view stresses military reasons: the Soviet armies were exhausted after advancing hundreds of kilometres in Bagration, and German panzer divisions had just defeated their spearhead north-east of the city. The other stresses Stalin's politics: he wanted no independent Polish authority in the capital, and for weeks refused Allied supply aircraft the use of Soviet airfields. Most historians accept that both played a part; they disagree on the balance.
- **Who lost at Arnhem?** — Montgomery called Market Garden '90 per cent successful'; others see it as an avoidable failure. Blame has been laid on the plan's single road, on intelligence warnings about SS panzer units near Arnhem that were discounted, on drop zones 10–13 km from the bridge, on radios that did not work, on the delay at Nijmegen and on the slow advance of XXX Corps. Major-General Sosabowski of the Polish Parachute Brigade, who had warned against the plan, was removed from command afterwards — a decision widely seen today as unjust.
- **Numbers murdered in the camps** — Early post-war figures for individual camps were often far too high: a Soviet commission put the dead of Auschwitz at 4 million, which stood on the memorial until 1990, and Majdanek's at 1.5 million. Research in the camp records, transport lists and population data has since produced lower, better-founded figures — about 1.1 million for Auschwitz (Franciszek Piper), about 78,000 for Majdanek (Tomasz Kranz). Treblinka estimates range from about 800,000 to 925,000. These revisions do not change the total of about six million Jews murdered, which rests on separate population studies.
- **Myths of 'the Few'** — Some 2,900 aircrew of Fighter Command took part in the Battle of Britain, and about 544 were killed. They were not only British: about one in five came from elsewhere, including Poles, Czechs, New Zealanders, Canadians and others, and the Polish No. 303 Squadron had the best record of any. Nor were they hopelessly outnumbered in fighters. Their success owed much to radar and the control system, to Hurricanes as much as Spitfires, and to German errors. Some historians argue that the Royal Navy, rather than Fighter Command alone, was the real barrier to invasion.

## Not fully verified (researchers' notes)

- **Fort Eben-Emael** — Coordinates are for the Belgian war memorial at the entrance on Rue du Fort (Block 1). French Wikipedia gives the fort as 50°47′49″N 5°40′47″E, i.e. the centre of the roof area about 500 m east. Ticket prices are not shown on the home page; check the visit page.
- **Mardasson Memorial, Bastogne** — No source opened gave coordinates for the star itself: the uswarmemorials.org point is for 'Mardasson Hill' and is effectively at the museum (about 100–200 m from the memorial). Editor may wish to refine with ABMC data (abmc.gov could not be fetched). ABMC has renamed it the Battle of the Bulge Monument; consider showing both names.
- **December 44 Historical Museum, La Gleize** — Tiger II on the square: TracesOfWar 50.410113, 5.846017. december44.com could not be read (robots block); opening days unverified.
- **Recogne German war cemetery** — The 'look' detail on stone crosses is from general knowledge of Volksbund cemeteries and was not confirmed on a source opened for this stop; the Volksbund page could not be fetched. Wikipedia gives 50.04833, 5.74083, matching TracesOfWar.
- **Henri-Chapelle American Cemetery** — abmc.gov returned errors to every fetch, so hours follow the standard ABMC pattern and were not confirmed for 2026. - **Luxembourg American Cemetery, Hamm** — abmc.gov could not be fetched. The cemetery address is 50 Val du Scheid, L-2517 Luxembourg.
- **National Museum of Military History, Diekirch** — TracesOfWar gives 49.870992, 6.159558 (Bamertal 10). Chronicle.lu says the museum was started by volunteers in 1982; Wikipedia and Liberation Route say 1984. Hours unverified: mnhm.lu blocks automated reading.
- **RAF Manston Spitfire & Hurricane Memorial Museum** — Coordinates come from a directory site's map pin (picniq.co.uk), as no official or Wikipedia coordinate for the museum building was found; the RAF Manston Wikipedia airfield reference point is 51°20′53″N 1°21′00″E. - **Dunkirk 1940 Museum (Bastion 32)** — Museum's own site could not be fetched (robots block). Hours/prices from the Dunkirk tourist office sheet (marked 'unsecured') and the 2026 remembrance guide PDF, which disagree on the closing season.
- **East Mole (Jetée de l'Est), Dunkirk** — The jetty has been rebuilt and repaired since 1940; I could not confirm how much of the 1940 structure survives, so the text avoids that claim.

- **Auschwitz I (main camp), Oświęcim** — BOOKING RULES (auschwitz.org, checked Oct 2026): (1) Admission to the grounds is free, but every visitor needs a personalised entry card; the number is limited. (2) Since 1 March 2026 entry cards are available ONLY online at visit.auschwitz.org, the only official system; none are sold at the main entrance, and the museum does not work with outside resellers. (3) Book up to about three months ahead; free guide-less passes have been bookable 90 to 7 days before the visit since May 2025. (4) For most of the day a guide-educator is compulsory: guide-free entry is allowed only late in the day (e.g. from 17:00 April–September, from 14:00 in January and November). Guided tours (paid; prices on visit.auschwitz.org) last about 3.5 hours, start at Auschwitz I and continue to Birkenau; offered in Polish, English, French, German, Italian, Spanish and Russian, more in summer; online sales close 15 minutes before a tour. (5) Arrive at least 30 minutes early for security. Bags no larger than 35 x 25 x 15 cm. Dress appropriately. (6) Last entry varies by month (14:00 in December to 19:00 in June–August); visitors may stay 90 minutes after last entry. (7) Closed 1 January, 25 December and Easter Sunday. Some areas (parts of Birkenau, part of Block 5, the Russian exhibition in Block 14) are temporarily closed. A free shuttle bus runs between Auschwitz I and Birkenau (per Wikivoyage). Coordinates are Wikipedia's Google Earth point for the museum entrance (labelled as such in the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum article). Oświęcim is about 60 km west of Kraków.
